# Iceberg Model

Use to investigate recurring events through patterns, structures, and assumptions.
Supply incident history or observations across time. A single event cannot establish
a recurring pattern.

## Procedure

1. Record the event without attributing blame.
2. Compare earlier events, including counterexamples, to identify a supported pattern.
3. Propose structures that could produce it: incentives, constraints, information
   flows, interfaces, or resource allocation. Specify evidence that would test each.
4. Examine documented beliefs that support those structures. Unstated beliefs remain
   hypotheses to discuss with people, never facts about their motives.
5. Choose the deepest intervention justified by evidence, cost, and authorization.

Output four levels with evidence status and a proposed intervention or check. Stop
at the deepest supported level. A temporary fix can be appropriate while a deeper
explanation remains untested; do not force a cultural explanation for a simple defect.

## Example and boundary

Three event cancellations followed late room bookings. Records establish timing,
but not the reason. A missing booking owner is a structural hypothesis; inspect the
handoff before recommending ownership changes. "Nobody values planning" is not an
observed mental model. With only one cancellation, ask for history or keep patterns unknown.
